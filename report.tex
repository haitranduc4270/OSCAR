\begin{abstract}
The precise classification of cancer subtypes is a cornerstone of precision oncology, yet the integration of heterogeneous multi-omic data remains a significant computational challenge. Existing deep learning frameworks often utilize symmetric fusion strategies that treat omic modalities as independent, parallel inputs, thereby overlooking the directional regulatory dependencies inherent in biological systems. We propose \textbf{OSCAR} (\textbf{O}rder-aware \textbf{S}elf-\textbf{C}ross \textbf{A}ttention \textbf{R}epresentation), a novel hierarchical deep learning framework that explicitly incorporates the biological ``Central Dogma'' flow: $\text{Genomics} \rightarrow \text{Transcriptomics} \rightarrow \text{Proteomics}$. Each modality is encoded by an MLP into a shared latent. Ordered cross-attention uses the mRNA latent as the query for the CNV latent and the proteomic latent as the query for the mRNA latent. Gated fusion combines each updated latent with the encoder latent of the same modality, after which self-attention is applied to the three omic tokens. On BRCA the gate values remain approximately $0.5$, and the classifier therefore receives both latents. We evaluated OSCAR on three benchmark cohorts from The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA): breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA), lung cancer (LUNG), and colorectal adenocarcinoma (COADREAD). Our model achieves state-of-the-art performance, reaching an accuracy of \textbf{91.27\%} in the high-heterogeneity BRCA dataset and \textbf{96.71\%} in LUNG subtype classification, outperforming established graph-based and symmetric attention baselines. Furthermore, five-fold consensus input-gradient saliency on BRCA, checked against differential abundance and pathway enrichment, is consistent with known luminal mitotic programs and the 17q12/\textit{ERBB2} amplicon. \textit{TBC1D31} and \textit{GAS2L3} are additional high-ranking features and are reported as unconfirmed nominations. These gradients rank input features of the trained classifier. They do not establish directional regulation, causal cross-omic effects, or new clinical biomarkers. The classification gains are evaluated separately, through the cohort comparison and the order ablation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
\IEEEPARstart{T}{he} TCGA Pan-Cancer analyses have established molecular cancer classification as a central component of precision oncology, supporting prognosis, treatment stratification, and biomarker discovery, as framed by The Cancer Genome Atlas (TCGA) \cite{TCGA2013} and the Pan-Cancer Atlas \cite{Hoadley2018PanCancer}. Cancer is inherently heterogeneous; tumors arising in the same organ can follow markedly different clinical trajectories because they are driven by distinct molecular programs. Although histopathology remains clinically important, high-throughput profiling has shifted subtype discovery toward molecular signatures derived from genomics, transcriptomics, epigenomics, and proteomics. Large-scale TCGA studies of breast, lung, and colorectal tumors have shown that molecular subtypes often refine or complement tissue-level diagnosis, including TCGA breast characterization \cite{Koboldt2012BRCA}, LUAD \cite{TCGA2014LUAD}, LUSC \cite{TCGA2012LUSC}, and COADREAD \cite{TCGA2012COADREAD}. 

Recent reviews \cite{Cai2022Review, CarrilloPerez2022, Abdelaziz2024Review, Han2025Review} have emphasized that multi-omic integration is now central to cancer research because single-omic views rarely capture the layered regulatory mechanisms that connect genotype to phenotype. Integrating complementary modalities such as Copy Number Variations (CNV), messenger RNA (mRNA), and Reverse Phase Protein Array (RPPA) is therefore essential for a more faithful representation of oncogenic processes and subtype-specific biology \cite{CrossAttOmics2024, Omran2025}. 

Over the past decade, the computational strategies for multi-omic fusion have evolved through distinct technological paradigms. Early methodologies primarily relied on matrix factorization and joint statistical modeling—such as iCluster \cite{iCluster2009}, integrative NMF \cite{IntNMF2017}, and JIVE \cite{JIVE2013}—or simple feature concatenation extended by deep networks like OmiEmbed \cite{OmiEmbed2021} and DeepMO \cite{DeepMO2020}. While providing a rigorous statistical foundation, these early methods frequently struggle with non-linear cross-modal dependencies and are highly vulnerable to scale mismatches and noise propagation. To capture cross-omic structure, later models adopted graph-based architectures; models like MOGONET \cite{MOGONET2021}, MoGCN \cite{MoGCN2022}, DeepMoIC \cite{DeepMoIC2024}, and $M^2$CGCN \cite{M2CGCN2025} leverage patient similarity networks or feature graphs to refine inter-omic correlations. Concurrently, the success of Transformers inspired attention-based frameworks—including moBRCA-net \cite{moBRCAnat2023}, CrossAttOmics \cite{CrossAttOmics2024}, MoxGATE \cite{MoxGATE2023}, and the Multi-Omics Transformer (MOT) \cite{MOT2023}—which utilize symmetric cross-attention or gated mechanics to adaptively align heterogeneous features, while generative or pathway-guided networks \cite{SubtypeGAN2020, SurvPath2023} attempted to constrain these latent representations using biological priors.

Despite substantial methodological progress, a key conceptual limitation persists across these modern paradigms. Most existing models, whether graph-based, attention-driven, or generative, still treat multi-omic modalities as symmetric or parallel inputs. This ``flat" fusion assumption overlooks the directional regulatory logic summarized by the Central Dogma, in which genomic alterations influence transcript abundance and transcripts subsequently shape protein-level outcomes. In practice, CNVs, mRNA expression, and RPPA measurements are not merely correlated feature blocks; they are connected by sequential dependencies. Consequently, even state-of-the-art (SOTA) methods may miss subtype-discriminative signals that depend on cross-layer ordering rather than on undirected statistical similarity alone. Furthermore, this limitation propagates into model explainability. Traditional post-hoc tools (e.g., SHAP, LIME) or modern symmetric attention weights \cite{moBRCAnat2023, OmiEmbed2021} evaluate features either in isolation or as undirected correlations, failing to capture the directional, cascading nature of cross-omic interactions.

To address this gap, we propose OSCAR (Order-aware Self-Cross Attention Representation), a deep learning architecture that explicitly incorporates biological hierarchy through an Ordered Cross-Attention (OCA) mechanism. Unlike recent integration models that treat modalities as parallel inputs, OSCAR introduces a directional inductive bias aligned with the regulatory flow from CNV to mRNA to RPPA. Each modality is first compressed by an MLP. Ordered cross-attention then updates the mRNA latent from the CNV latent and the proteomic latent from the mRNA latent. Self-attention is then applied to the resulting omic tokens. This design grounds the interaction in a biologically plausible sequence that directly motivates the present study.

A gated fusion then mixes each cross-attention update with the encoder latent of the same modality. The primary contributions of this work are three-fold:
\begin{enumerate}
\item We propose OSCAR, an order-aware self-cross attention framework for multi-omic fusion that follows the directional flow of biological information.
\item We demonstrate that our model achieves better performance across three diverse TCGA cohorts: breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA), lung cancer (LUAD/LUSC, collectively denoted as LUNG), and colorectal adenocarcinoma (COAD/READ, denoted as COADREAD), particularly in resolving high-heterogeneity subtypes.
\item We report a BRCA input-gradient analysis with cross-fold consensus, differential abundance filtering, and pathway enrichment, used as a consistency check against known luminal and HER2 programs.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Materials and Methods}

\subsection{Data Preparation}
We evaluated OSCAR on three TCGA multi-omic cohorts: breast invasive carcinoma (BRCA), lung cancer (LUNG)---comprising lung adenocarcinoma (LUAD) and lung squamous cell carcinoma (LUSC)---and colorectal adenocarcinoma (COADREAD). Each cohort includes matched genomic copy number variation (CNV), transcriptomic mRNA expression, proteomic reverse phase protein array (RPPA), and clinical subtype annotations. These cohorts were selected because their molecular landscapes and subtype definitions have been extensively characterized by the TCGA consortium, including benchmark studies on BRCA \cite{Koboldt2012BRCA}, LUAD \cite{TCGA2014LUAD}, LUSC \cite{TCGA2012LUSC}, and COADREAD \cite{TCGA2012COADREAD}. Molecular and clinical data were obtained from the UCSC Xena platform \cite{Goldman2020Xena}, with proteomic profiles supplemented from The Cancer Proteome Atlas (TCPA) \cite{Li2013TCPA}.

During preprocessing, we retained only patients shared across CNV, mRNA, RPPA, and clinical metadata to ensure complete multi-omic alignment. Samples without subtype labels and features containing missing values were removed. The resulting cohorts are a high-dimensional, small-sample classification setting, with 39,076 to 45,486 molecular features and fewer than 700 aligned patients per dataset, as summarized together with the subtype distributions in Table \ref{tab:dataset_summary}.

\subsection{Problem Formulation}
Let the input space be defined by three distinct omic modalities from the same patient: genomic data (CNV) $X_{G} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_G}$, transcriptomic data (mRNA) $X_{T} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_T}$, and proteomic data (RPPA) $X_{P} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_P}$. Given a training set of $N$ samples, our goal is to learn a mapping $f: \{X_G, X_T, X_P\} \rightarrow \mathcal{Y}$, where $\mathcal{Y}$ corresponds to the ground-truth cancer subtype labels.

\subsection{Per-omic Encoding}
Each modality is mapped to a shared latent of dimension $d=128$ by a ReLU MLP with a 512-unit hidden layer,
\begin{equation}
    Z_i = \mathrm{MLP}(X_i), \qquad i \in \{G, T, P\}.
\end{equation}
$Z_G$, $Z_T$, and $Z_P$ are single vectors per patient. No attention is applied inside a modality, so the encoder does not model gene--gene interactions.

\subsection{Ordered Cross-Attention (OCA)}
The defining step of OSCAR is an ordered update of these vectors. Unlike symmetric fusion models such as MoGCN \cite{MoGCN2022} or MoxGATE \cite{MoxGATE2023}, the update follows the Central Dogma direction, and it is an element-wise gate on the latent rather than a softmax over genes.

The mRNA latent is the query and the CNV latent supplies key and value. With layer-normalized projections $Q_T$, $K_G$, and $V_G$,
\begin{equation}
    g_{T} = \sigma\!\left(W_{T}[Q_T \parallel K_G]\right), \qquad
    H_T = Q_T + g_{T} \odot V_G.
\end{equation}
A residual feed-forward block is then applied to $H_T$. The proteomic latent queries the mRNA latent in the same form,
\begin{equation}
    g_{P} = \sigma\!\left(W_{P}[Q_P \parallel K_T]\right), \qquad
    H_P = Q_P + g_{P} \odot V_T.
\end{equation}
In the BRCA configuration, $K_T$ and $V_T$ are taken from the encoder mRNA latent $Z_T$. LUNG and COADREAD use the mRNA latent after the CNV update. The CNV latent $Z_G$ is not rewritten by either step.

\subsection{Gated Fusion}
For mRNA and proteomics, a second gate mixes the cross-attention update with the encoder latent:
\begin{equation}
    \alpha_i = \sigma\!\left(W_g[Z_i \parallel H_i]\right), \qquad
    F_i = \alpha_i \odot H_i + (1-\alpha_i) \odot Z_i,
\end{equation}
where $i \in \{T, P\}$, $\parallel$ denotes concatenation, and $\odot$ denotes element-wise multiplication. A value of $\alpha_i$ near $0.5$ retains both the ordered update and the modality-specific latent. The CNV representation passed to the next stage is $Z_G$.

\subsection{Token Self-Attention and Classification}
The three latents are then treated as tokens. A learned pool token is prepended and the sequence is passed through a two-layer Transformer encoder \cite{Vaswani2017Attention} with 8 heads:
\begin{equation}
    [u; \tilde{Z}_G; \tilde{F}_T; \tilde{F}_P]
    = \mathrm{Transformer}([u_0; Z_G; F_T; F_P]).
\end{equation}
The classifier uses only the pool token,
\begin{equation}
    \hat{y} = \mathrm{Softmax}(\mathrm{MLP}(u)).
\end{equation}
The model is optimized with cross-entropy. Self-attention therefore mixes the three omic tokens after the ordered update. Because this operation acts on omic tokens rather than on individual genes, the feature ranking below uses input gradients.

\subsection{Experimental Setup}
For model evaluation, we applied stratified 5-fold cross-validation to each cohort so that subtype proportions were preserved across folds. In each iteration, one fold was held out for testing and the remaining four folds were used for training. Reported performance values are averaged across the five runs, reducing dependence on any single train-test split. To evaluate the model’s performance, we report Accuracy (Acc) and F1 Score (F1).

Each modality was encoded into a shared latent space of $d=128$ via a ReLU MLP with a 512-dimensional hidden layer. Self-attention over omic tokens used 8 heads ($d_k=16$) and 2 Transformer layers. The classifier was a ReLU MLP with a 128-unit hidden layer and dropout 0.4. Models were trained on an NVIDIA Tesla V100 GPU for up to 200 epochs using AdamW, learning rate $2 \times 10^{-4}$, weight decay $1 \times 10^{-5}$, batch size 32 and Cross-Entropy loss.

The OSCAR framework, visualized in Figure \ref{fig:model}, integrates multi-omic data in four stages: per-omic MLP encoding, ordered cross-attention, gated fusion, and self-attention over the omic tokens.

\subsubsection{Interpretability Analysis}
To read which input features the trained BRCA classifier uses, we computed input-gradient saliency on held-out validation folds using the fold-wise best checkpoint.
Specifically, we attributed the true-class logit by the mean absolute gradient $\mathrm{Mean}\,|\partial\mathrm{logit}/\partial x|$ with respect to each CNV, mRNA, and RPPA input feature under evaluation mode.
Features that appeared in the per-omic top-$20$ in at least three of five folds were retained as consensus candidates ($51$ features on BRCA).
We further assessed one-versus-rest differential abundance on the full cohort ($N=511$) with a Mann--Whitney $U$ test, Benjamini--Hochberg FDR \cite{Benjamini1995FDR} within each omic$\times$class consensus set, and Cohen's $d$.
A consensus feature passed this filter when $q<0.05$ and $|d|\ge 0.3$ ($45/51$ features).
Over-representation used Enrichr \cite{Kuleshov2016Enrichr} with GO Biological Process 2025 \cite{Ashburner2000GO}, KEGG 2021 Human \cite{Kanehisa2000KEGG}, and MSigDB Hallmark 2020 \cite{Liberzon2015Hallmark}.

We present the main comparative results in Table \ref{tab:results}. Overall, the proposed OSCAR framework with Gated Fusion delivers the strongest and most consistent performance across the three benchmark cohorts. Importantly, OSCAR is not only competitive on a single preferred metric, but remains robust across both F1 score and accuracy, indicating that the model balances class-sensitive performance with global predictive reliability.



Several findings deserve emphasis. First, the results on BRCA provide the clearest evidence of the value of hierarchical multi-omic modeling. BRCA is the most heterogeneous cohort in our study, comprising four molecularly distinct subtypes with overlapping biological signatures. In this challenging setting, our model achieves the best overall performance, reaching 91.36\% F1 and 91.27\% accuracy. Compared with the specialized moBRCA-net \cite{moBRCAnat2023}, OSCAR improves the F1 score by 2.21\% and the accuracy by 2.73\%. It also surpasses the more general graph-based baseline MoGCN \cite{MoGCN2022} by even larger margins. These gains are important because BRCA is precisely the kind of dataset in which subtle cross-omic dependencies matter most; the improvement suggests that explicitly respecting the biological progression from genomics to transcriptomics and proteomics helps the model discriminate among closely related subtypes more effectively than flat fusion strategies.

Second, the method demonstrates strong generalization across different cancer types. On the LUNG cohort, OSCAR achieves 96.76\% F1 and 96.71\% accuracy, outperforming all competing methods on both metrics. This result is particularly notable because it shows that the proposed directional inductive bias does not overfit to one tissue context or one subtype taxonomy. Instead, it appears to capture a more general integration principle that remains effective even when the molecular landscape changes. The relatively small standard deviations on LUNG further indicate that the gains are stable across repeated runs rather than being driven by a favorable split or isolated experimental fluctuation.

Third, on COADREAD, OSCAR reaches 93.14\% F1 and 92.34\% accuracy, compared with 92.16\% and 91.67\% for MoxGATE \cite{MoxGATE2023}. The margins are 0.98 and 0.67 points, while the fold standard deviations are about 3--4 points, so the differences on this cohort lie within the fold-to-fold variation.

Across cohorts, OSCAR attains the highest F1 and accuracy. The margin is largest on BRCA and smallest on COADREAD. The order ablation below relates this pattern to the CNV $\rightarrow$ mRNA $\rightarrow$ RPPA direction.

\subsection{Ablation Study}


To rigorously evaluate the contribution of each module within our framework, we conducted a series of ablation experiments on the BRCA and LUNG datasets. These experiments focus on two aspects: the architectural components and the hierarchical sequence of the attention mechanism.

\subsubsection{Importance of Model Components}

The comparisons above show that OSCAR outperforms the symmetric fusion baselines on all three cohorts, and that the Central Dogma order outperforms the reverse and mixed orders (Table \ref{tab:ablation_order}). \subsubsection{Input Features Used by the Classifier}

Pathway coherence was assessed by Enrichr over-representation on these gene lists.

\paragraph{Recovery of established subtype programs.}
As summarized in Fig.~\ref{fig:combined_saliency}, the highest-ranked features fall in biologically recognized programs.
Those programs were not supplied as training labels:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{Luminal A and B.}
    Consensus features are enriched for mitotic apparatus and cell-cycle genes
    (e.g.\ \textit{ASPM}, \textit{CEP55}, \textit{CENPE}, \textit{NUF2}, \textit{DLGAP5}, \textit{CDKN3}, \textit{KIF11},
    \textit{GTSE1}, \textit{SGO1}), in line with proliferation-oriented distinctions among luminal tumors
    \cite{Gao2018LuminalA,Ades2014LuminalB,PerezPena2017MitoticLumA,Ibrahim2024ASPM}.
    Hallmark and GO Biological Process over-representation on this list names a cell-cycle vocabulary (Table~\ref{tab:luma_enrichment}) \cite{Malorni2016RBsig,Liberzon2015Hallmark}.
    KEGG terms for this $16$-gene list do not pass FDR.
    Over-representation treats the list as an unordered gene set, so these terms name a cell-cycle vocabulary and do not themselves say whether the transcripts are higher or lower in Luminal~A.
    With $5$--$16$ genes per subtype, the enrichment supports that vocabulary for the Luminal~A list rather than a genome-wide pathway screen.
    Differential abundance clarifies polarity (Fig.~\ref{fig:cohens_d}): mitotic transcripts are systematically lower in Luminal~A
    (Cohen's $d$ from about $-1.7$ to $-2.1$; \textit{SGO1} $d=-2.14$, \textit{CEP55} $d=-2.12$) and higher in Luminal~B
    (about $+0.6$ to $+0.9$), matching the more proliferative luminal phenotype
    \cite{Ades2014LuminalB}.
    At the protein layer, INPP4B is higher in Luminal~A ($d=+1.05$), consistent with its expression in ER-positive luminal cells and its frequent loss in basal-like carcinomas \cite{Fedele2010INPP4B,Gewinner2009INPP4B}.
    P53 protein is lower ($d=-0.78$), in line with the low \textit{TP53}-mutation rate of Luminal~A relative to basal-like disease \cite{Koboldt2012BRCA}.
    EEF2 protein is also lower ($d=-0.80$); the measurement is reported as an effect size.

    \item \textit{HER2-enriched.}
    Consensus features are dominated by the 17q12/\textit{ERBB2} amplicon neighborhood at both CNV and mRNA levels
    (e.g.\ \textit{STARD3}, \textit{TCAP}, \textit{MIEN1}, \textit{GRB7}, \textit{PGAP3}, \textit{PNMT}, \textit{CDK12}, \textit{GSDMB}),
    consistent with genomic characterization of HER2-positive disease
    \cite{Staaf2010HER2,Kao2006Amplicon,Sahlberg2013HER2Amplicon}.
    Twenty of the $21$ HER2 consensus features pass differential abundance.
    Five symbols are consensus features in both CNV and mRNA, with the same sign and large effects
    (Fig.~\ref{fig:her2_concordance}): \textit{STARD3} ($d_{\mathrm{CNV}}=1.87$, $d_{\mathrm{mRNA}}=2.40$),
    \textit{MIEN1} ($1.94$, $2.25$), \textit{TCAP} ($1.87$, $2.18$), \textit{GRB7} ($1.94$, $2.15$), and \textit{PNMT} ($1.87$, $1.79$).
    This CNV$\cap$mRNA concordance supports cis-acting amplification--expression coupling at 17q12 \cite{Pollack2002Cis,Staaf2010HER2,Kao2006Amplicon}.

    \item \textit{Basal-like.}
    Only $4$ of $9$ basal consensus features pass differential abundance.
    The supported signals are \textit{MIA} mRNA (higher vs.\ rest, $d=+2.33$), \textit{PGAP3} mRNA ($d=-1.15$),
    GATA3 protein ($d=-2.24$), and phospho-TUBERIN/TSC2 ($d=-0.44$).
    Low GATA3 protein agrees with its function as a luminal epithelial transcription factor that is reduced in high-grade, hormone-receptor-negative carcinomas \cite{Mehra2005GATA3}.
    \textit{PGAP3} belongs to the 17q12 amplicon \cite{Kao2006Amplicon,Staaf2010HER2}, so its lower abundance outside HER2-enriched tumors is the counterpart of that gain rather than a separate basal program.
    \textit{MIA} carries the largest of these basal effect sizes, and phospho-TUBERIN/TSC2 the smallest.
    Both remain measured attributions, pending a subtype-specific reading.
    Copy-number features from the \textit{ERBB2} neighborhood that are gradient-stable for this class
    (\textit{ERBB2}, \textit{GRB7}, \textit{TCAP}, \textit{PGAP3}, \textit{PPP1R1B}) do not pass the abundance filter.
    The basal list is therefore mixed rather than a single amplicon program, consistent with the molecular diversity of basal-like disease
    \cite{Koboldt2012BRCA}. These are features the classifier uses, and they suggest a mixed basal reading rather than one program.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Additional high-ranking genes.}
Beyond the programs above, the same ranking also places weight on genes that are less often used to name these subtypes. Their reported functions suggest a direction for a later reading:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textit{TBC1D31} (mRNA) was consensus-stable for both Luminal~A (decreased vs.\ rest, $d=-1.39$, $3/5$ folds) and Luminal~B
    (increased vs.\ rest, $d=+0.91$, $4/5$ folds).
    TBC1D31 participates in a centrosomal complex that controls primary ciliogenesis \cite{Senatore2021TBC1D31}.
    A separate study links the broader TBC1-domain family, and \textit{TBC1D7} in particular, to a glycolytic phenotype in triple-negative breast cancer \cite{Lupi2024TBC1D}.
    The Luminal~A/B contrast is a rank from the true-class logit. The ciliogenesis function suggests one direction in which that contrast could be read.
    The cited \textit{TBC1D7} result remains a finding about a different gene in triple-negative disease.

    \item \textit{GAS2L3} (mRNA) was a strong Luminal~A consensus feature ($d=-1.56$, $q=1.7\times 10^{-47}$ inside the Luminal~A mRNA consensus set).
    GAS2L3 is a DREAM/E2F-regulated cytokinesis gene required for abscission and genomic stability
    \cite{Wolter2012GAS2L3,Peer2013GAS2L3}. That function sits next to the Hallmark E2F / G2--M terms for Luminal~A
    and suggests a cytokinesis reading of the same cell-cycle list. It does not establish \textit{GAS2L3} as a subtype marker.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Summary.}
Input-gradient saliency ranks features of the trained classifier.
Agreement with luminal proliferation and the HER2 amplicon is a consistency check.
That ranking does not show that ordered cross-attention learned directional regulation, does not identify causal cross-omic effects, and does not establish \textit{TBC1D31} or \textit{GAS2L3} as new clinical biomarkers.

\subsubsection{Gated Fusion across BRCA Subtypes}
High mRNA abundance does not always produce a matching protein level, because post-transcriptional regulation intervenes \cite{Liu2016mRNAProtein}.
The gated fusion is the component that can weigh the ordered-cross-attention latent $H_i$ against the modality-specific latent $Z_i$.

On BRCA this gate was evaluated on held-out validation folds, using each fold's best checkpoint.
The gate is a $128$-dimensional vector.
Fig.~\ref{fig:gates} summarizes, for each subtype, the mean across latent dimensions and the fraction of dimensions above $0.5$, averaged over the five folds.
Mean gates are approximately $0.5$ for every subtype: about $0.52$--$0.54$ for mRNA and $0.50$ for RPPA.
The fraction of dimensions above $0.5$ is about $0.56$--$0.58$ for mRNA and $0.51$--$0.53$ for RPPA, and the fold-to-fold spread overlaps Luminal~A, Luminal~B, Basal-like, and HER2-enriched tumors.
A mean weight near $0.5$ indicates that the learned gate retains both the ordered-cross-attention representation and the modality-specific latent for every BRCA subtype.
Removing the gate lowers accuracy (Table~\ref{tab:ablation_components}).

\section{Conclusions and Discussions}

In this study, we proposed OSCAR (Order-aware Self-Cross Attention Representation), a hierarchical deep learning framework for multi-omic cancer subtype classification that incorporates the biological information flow from genomics (CNV) to transcriptomics (mRNA) and proteomics (RPPA). Unlike conventional multi-omic integration approaches that treat different modalities as symmetric or parallel inputs, OSCAR employs ordered cross-attention to model interactions between successive omic layers, gated fusion to balance modality-specific and cross-omic representations, and self-attention to integrate the resulting omic representations for classification.

Experimental evaluations on three TCGA cohorts demonstrated the effectiveness of the proposed framework. Under stratified five-fold cross-validation, OSCAR achieved accuracies of 91.27\%, 96.71\%, and 92.34\% on BRCA, LUNG, and COADREAD, respectively, with corresponding F1 scores of 91.36\%, 96.76\%, and 93.14\%. OSCAR outperformed the evaluated graph-based and attention-based baselines across all three cohorts. The improvement was particularly notable on BRCA, where the proposed method achieved an accuracy of 91.27\%, compared with 89.73\% for MoxGATE, 88.54\% for moBRCA-net, and 86.91\% for MoGCN. These results suggest that explicitly incorporating an ordered cross-omic inductive bias can improve subtype discrimination, particularly in heterogeneous cancer cohorts. Nevertheless, the relatively small performance margins on COADREAD, together with the fold-to-fold variability, indicate that the magnitude of improvement differs across datasets and should be interpreted in the context of the experimental evaluation.

The ablation experiments further clarified the contributions of individual architectural components. Removing self-attention reduced accuracy from 91.27\% to 87.12\% on BRCA, from 96.71\% to 94.88\% on LUNG, and from 92.34\% to 90.45\% on COADREAD. Replacing gated fusion with a linear projection also decreased accuracy across all three cohorts. Furthermore, the ordered cross-attention experiments consistently favored the biological sequence CNV $\rightarrow$ mRNA $\rightarrow$ RPPA over the alternative mixed and reverse orderings. On BRCA, reversing the sequence reduced accuracy by approximately 4.83 percentage points, from 91.27\% to 86.44\%. Collectively, these findings support the utility of both cross-omic ordering and subsequent token-level integration within OSCAR. However, the ordering experiments establish the predictive value of this architectural choice rather than demonstrating that the model has learned causal regulatory relationships between omic layers.

The interpretability analysis on BRCA provided an additional biological consistency check. Cross-fold input-gradient saliency identified features associated with established subtype-specific molecular programs. For Luminal A and Luminal B tumors, the consensus features were enriched for cell-cycle and mitotic processes, consistent with differences in proliferative activity between these subtypes. For HER2-enriched tumors, several CNV and mRNA features within the 17q12/\textit{ERBB2} amplicon neighborhood showed concordant increases, supporting the consistency of the model's feature rankings with known molecular characteristics. The Basal-like feature profile was more heterogeneous, reflecting a mixture of signals rather than a single dominant program. The analysis also identified \textit{TBC1D31} and \textit{GAS2L3} as additional high-ranking features that may warrant further investigation. Importantly, gradient saliency identifies features used by the trained classifier; it does not establish directional regulation, causal cross-omic effects, or novel clinical biomarkers. Independent validation is therefore necessary before these additional candidates can be assigned biomarker significance.
